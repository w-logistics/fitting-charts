Hydraulic Fitting Torque Chart

Assembly torque by dash size for JIC 37° flare (SAE J514), ORFS face seal (SAE J1453), and SAE O-ring boss ports (SAE J1926 / J514), plus flats from wrench resistance (FFWR) where the fitting maker prints that method.

Use the fitting maker's sheet. The numbers below are what Parker printed, and one JIC flats table is what Tompkins printed. Another brand, another plating, or another material (steel, stainless, or brass) can call for a different torque on the same dash. Oil on the threads changes the clamp load a torque wrench produces. If the maker of the fitting in your hand publishes a value, that value comes first. This page does not convert a printed foot-pound into newton-meters, or the other way around. A unit is shown only when that source prints it.

JIC 37° flare torque and FFWR

These are Parker Triple-Lok values from bulletin 4300-DT, Table 14 (December 2004). Parker says two methods are acceptable for a 37° flare, and that flats from wrench resistance is the preferred one. How Parker counts the flats

  1. Align the tube flare against the nose. Tighten the nut lightly so the flare is clamped between the nose and the sleeve. Parker says a light wrench pull of no more than 30 in-lb may be needed to reach that point. That pull is the wrench-resistance position. It is not the final torque.
  2. From there, turn the nut the number of hex flats in the table. Tompkins describes one flat as one side of the hex: one sixth of a turn, or 60 degrees. Parker's 2-1/2 on a -4 tube nut is two flats plus half a flat.
  3. Mark the nut and the hex next to it at the wrench-resistance position before you turn, so the count is visible. Parker recommends a reference mark at the finished position as a check that the joint was tightened.
  4. Use a torque wrench only when the plating is consistent and known. Parker says the torque method should not be used with dissimilar plating, or when the plating is unknown. Plating, oil, and surface finish change how much clamp load a given torque produces. FFWR counts nut rotation instead, which is why Parker prefers it on a 37° flare.

Parker also notes that over-tightening a 37° flare can collapse the nose. A collapsed nose can cut flow and leak.

SAE O-ring boss port torque

These are Parker Table R1, SAE J1926 straight-thread port assembly, from Parker's port-end assembly guide. The allowance is +10% -0. Parker says to lubricate the threads before assembly. The values are for plated steel fittings in steel ports. For stainless, Parker says to use the upper limit of that range. For brass and aluminum, Parker says to decrease the torque by 35%. Those adjusted numbers are not printed here.

A non-adjustable stud is a straight fitting. Screw it in and tighten the stud. An adjustable stud is a shaped fitting with a locknut and a backup washer. Back the locknut off, keep the washer up against it, and screw the stud in until the washer meets the port face. Unscrew no more than one full turn to aim the tube end. Hold the body with a second wrench and tighten the locknut. Check that the O-ring is not pinched and that the washer sits flat on the port.

Heavy duty is the Seal-Lok stud, SAE J1926-2. Light duty is the Triple-Lok, Ferulok, and adapter stud, SAE J1926-3, which is the J514 side of this port. The port thread at each dash is the thread on the ORB chart and the JIC chart. The boss O-ring, not the thread, makes the seal. Parker does not list a heavy-duty stud on dash -2 or -3. From dash -12 up, the four columns are the same number.

Small dashes are printed as inch-pounds in parentheses, with N·m beside them. From dash -10 up, Parker prints foot-pounds and N·m. This table shows the unit Parker printed. It does not convert inch-pounds into foot-pounds. The same Parker table also lists hollow-hex and hex-head plug torques. Those plug columns are not copied here.

FAQ

Whose torque number should I use?

Use the fitting maker's published value for that part, material, and plating. The main tables on this page are Parker's. The second JIC flats table is Tompkins. Steel, stainless steel, and brass are not the same number. Plating and oil on the threads change the clamp load you get from a torque wrench. If your sheet and this page disagree, follow your sheet.

How do I count flats from wrench resistance on a JIC nut?

Parker prefers flats from wrench resistance for a 37° flare. Align the flare on the nose of the fitting and tighten lightly. Parker says a light wrench pull of no more than 30 in-lb may be needed to clamp the flare between the nose and the sleeve. From that position, turn the nut the number of hex flats in the Parker table. Tompkins describes one flat as one side of the hex, which is one sixth of a turn, or 60 degrees. Mark the nut and the fitting hex before you turn it so you can see the movement. Parker says not to use the torque method when the mating platings do not match, or when you do not know the plating, because oil and plating change the clamp load.

Does a JIC torque apply to an ORFS fitting of the same dash?

No. The seal is different, the thread at the same dash is different, and Parker prints a different table. A -8 JIC thread is 3/4-16. A -8 ORFS thread is 13/16-16. Parker prefers a torque wrench for an ORFS tube nut and prefers flats from wrench resistance for a JIC nut. The JIC chart and the ORFS chart list the threads.

What is the difference between an adjustable and a non-adjustable O-ring boss stud?

A non-adjustable stud is the straight fitting. Lubricate the O-ring, screw it into the port, and tighten the stud to the non-adjustable column. An adjustable stud is a shaped fitting with a locknut and a backup washer, so you can aim the elbow or tee. Screw it in until the washer meets the port face, back it off no more than one full turn, hold the body, and tighten the locknut to the adjustable column. Parker also splits each of those into a heavy-duty column (Seal-Lok, SAE J1926-2) and a light-duty column (Triple-Lok, Ferulok, and adapters, SAE J1926-3). On dash -4 those four numbers are not the same. From dash -12 up, Parker prints the same torque in all four columns.

Can I use the steel torque on stainless steel or brass?

Not as a straight copy. On the O-ring boss table, Parker says the values are for plated steel fittings in steel ports, with the threads lubricated. For stainless, Parker says to use the upper limit of the +10% -0 range. For brass and aluminum, Parker says to decrease the torque by 35%. This page does not print those reduced numbers. The 2004 JIC and ORFS tables print a +10% -0 tolerance and do not print a separate brass or stainless column. Use the note in the fitting maker's current catalog for the material you have.
