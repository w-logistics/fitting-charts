SAE O-Ring Boss (ORB) Fitting Chart

Straight-thread O-ring boss fittings for SAE J1926 ports: dash size, nominal tube OD, the UN or UNF thread, and the boss O-ring. The thread at each dash is the same thread the JIC 37° chart lists for SAE J514. The seal is not the flare. It is the O-ring at the base of the thread.

Quick rule: A -4 ORB is 7/16-20 UNF, for 1/4″ tube, and it uses O-ring 3-904 (AS568-904). The thread is straight. The O-ring sits at the shoulder. There is no taper and no flare. A -4 JIC fitting is the same thread with a 37° nose. A -4 ORFS fitting is a different thread, 9/16-18, with the O-ring in the flat face.

The dash is the nominal inch tube OD in sixteenths. Dash -8 is 8/16 in, which is 1/2 in tube. Parker’s port table also prints a metric tube size as a reference, and the footnote says any boss can be used for a tubing size depending on the rest of the design. Those reference sizes are 4 mm on -3, 6 mm on -4, 8 mm on -5, 10 mm on -6, 12 mm on -8, 14, 15, or 16 mm on -10, 18 or 20 mm on -12, 22 mm on -14, 25 or 28 mm on -16, 30, 32, or 35 mm on -20, 38 or 42 mm on -24, and 50 mm on -32. Dash -2 has no metric size in that column.

Sizes -2 through -10 are UNF. Sizes -12 and larger are 12-pitch UN. Parker’s port-detail table marks the internal thread class 2B (UNF-2B or UN-2B). The adapter section says the UN/UNF threads in that section are ANSI B1.1, class 2A or 2B. This chart leaves the class off the callout, the same way the JIC chart does. The thread OD columns are the basic major diameter of that callout (7/16 in is 0.4375 in). The millimeter column is that inch figure times 25.4, rounded to two decimal places. A remainder of 5 in the next digit is rounded up. It is the same set of diameters as the JIC chart, because the thread series is the same. It is not a separate metric thread.

Eaton’s identification chart prints a male outside diameter already rounded to two places: 0.31, 0.38, 0.44, 0.50, 0.56, 0.75, 0.88, 1.06, 1.19, 1.31, 1.63, 1.88, and 2.50 in, in dash order. Use those when you are matching Eaton’s card. Use the four-decimal column when you want the nominal major. A caliper on a male thread reads slightly under the nominal major. The female ID column is Eaton’s decimal for the inside of the port thread. Eaton also prints a fraction on that row. Use the decimal. On several rows the fraction is not the same number as the decimal (the -4 female row prints 13/32 and 0.39, and 13/32 in is 0.406 in).

The O-ring column is Parker’s 3-9xx number. The Parker O-ring handbook lists the same number as AS568A: 3-904 is AS568-904. The port table’s standard compound note says 90-durometer nitrile for hydraulic use. This chart has no hex column. On the SAE J1926-1 port-end table, column C4 for light duty (Triple-Lok, Ferulok, and adapters) is not the same as column C4 for heavy duty (Seal-Lok). A straight connector body can use another hex again. Match the hex to the shape in the catalog for the part in your hand.

How to identify ORB

Parker calls this port SAE straight thread. The standard on the port drawing is SAE J1926-1, also listed as ISO 11926-1. The female port is the O-ring boss. Eaton describes the male connector as a straight thread with an O-ring, and the female port as a straight thread, a machined spotface, and a chamfer that accepts the O-ring. The seal is the O-ring compressed into that chamfer. The threads hold the connection.

Parker’s port-detail table prints the chamfer angle as 12°, ±1°, on dash -2 through -6, and 15°, ±1°, on dash -8 through -32. That angle is the port, not a flare you measure on the male nose.

  1. Look at the thread. A male ORB thread is straight. The crests stay parallel. A taper toward the tip is NPT or BSPT, not this chart.
  2. Find the O-ring. It sits at the shoulder, at the base of the threads, under the hex or under a locknut. An adjustable elbow adds a locknut and a backup washer so the elbow can be aimed, and the O-ring is still at that port end. A 37° cone in front of the threads is JIC. An O-ring in a groove on a flat nose is ORFS.
  3. Measure the male outside diameter across the crests, and count threads per inch. Then use both numbers. Pitch alone collides: -2 and -3 are both 24 threads per inch, -4 and -5 are both 20, and -12 through -32 are all 12.
  4. Measure a female port across the thread inside diameter and use the female ID column. The port should show a spotface and a chamfer, not a pipe taper and not a 37° seat.
  5. Do not treat the thread as the seal. Parker’s straight-thread comparison lists no thread sealant as one of the reasons to use this port. A missing, cut, or flattened O-ring leaks even when the threads are tight. Replace the ring with the 3-9xx size for that dash, in a compound that suits the fluid.

How ORB differs from JIC, NPT, and ORFS

JIC (SAE J514 37° flare) uses this same dash and this same thread. Parker’s Triple-Lok 37° fittings are the J514 fitting. The male JIC nose is a 37° cone, and the seal is metal to metal. There is no boss O-ring. A JIC male will start into an ORB port of the same dash, because the thread fits, and the joint still has no O-ring in the chamfer. Match the end, not only the thread.

NPT is a tapered pipe thread. The name is a pipe size, not a tube OD, and the diameter changes along the thread. Parker’s pipe section says NPT needs a sealant because the crest and root leave a spiral path, and it points to a port with an elastomeric seal, such as SAE straight thread, when the joint is opened often or the pressure pulses. Dash -8 is the size that gets mixed up: ORB -8 is 3/4-16 straight, for 1/2 in tube. NPT -8 is 1/2-14 taper. The outside diameter of that NPT thread is about 0.84 in, not 0.75 in.

ORFS (SAE J1453) is also a straight thread and an O-ring, and it is a tube union rather than a port boss. The O-ring is in the flat face. The nut pulls two faces together. At the same dash the thread is one step larger than ORB on the sizes both charts list, except -32, where both callouts are 2-1/2-12 and the seals are still different. Some callouts appear in both families at different dashes. 9/16-18 is -6 ORB and -4 ORFS. 1-3/16-12 is -14 ORB and -12 ORFS.

BSPP is a 55° parallel pipe thread. On the fittings in Parker’s adapter section it seals on the spotface, with a retaining ring or another face seal, not in the SAE chamfer. Sizes and the G, R, Rp, and Rc names are on the BSP chart. A 24° metric cone is a different fitting again: see the DIN 2353 chart.

FAQ

How do I identify an ORB fitting?

Look for a straight thread that stays the same diameter from the first full thread to the last, with an O-ring or an O-ring groove at the shoulder, at the base of the threads. There is no taper toward the tip, no 37° flare, and no O-ring groove in a flat nose. Measure the male thread across the crests and count the threads per inch, then match both numbers to the chart. The thread on an ORB dash is the same thread as the JIC dash of that number, so the thread alone will not tell them apart.

How does ORB differ from JIC?

The thread matches and the seal does not. A -8 ORB and a -8 JIC are both 3/4-16 UNF for 1/2 in tube. JIC seals metal to metal on a 37° flare. ORB seals when the O-ring at the shoulder is compressed into the chamfer of a SAE J1926 port. A JIC male can screw into an ORB port and still leak, because the flare is not the sealing surface of that port.

How does ORB differ from NPT?

NPT is a tapered pipe thread. The diameter changes along the thread, and the joint is sealed on the threads, with a sealant. ORB is a straight UN or UNF thread. The threads hold the fitting, and the O-ring seals in the port chamfer. The dash numbers are not the same thread. Dash -8 ORB is 3/4-16. Dash -8 NPT is a 1/2-14 pipe thread. Do not install an NPT fitting in an ORB port.

How does ORB differ from ORFS?

Both use a straight thread and an O-ring, and the O-ring is not in the same place. On ORFS the O-ring sits in a groove on the flat nose, and a swivel nut draws that face against a flat seat. On ORB the O-ring sits at the shoulder and seals in a port. The thread at a given dash is not the same either. A -6 ORB is 9/16-18. A -6 ORFS is 11/16-16. The O-ring series is not the same. ORB uses the 900-series boss ring (3-906 on dash -6). ORFS uses a face-seal ring (2-012 on dash -6).

What O-ring does a -4 ORB fitting use?

Parker Catalog 4300 lists 3-904 for dash -4. The Parker O-ring handbook lists that size as AS568-904, with an inside diameter of 0.351 in (8.92 mm) and a width of 0.072 in (1.83 mm). The standard compound in the fitting catalog is 90-durometer nitrile. That is not the ORFS face-seal ring. Dash -4 ORFS uses 2-011.
