BSPP and BSPT Thread Size Chart (ISO 228-1 and ISO 7-1)

Nominal pipe sizes from 1/16″ through 2″, with threads per inch and the major (outside) diameter used to name the size. BSPP is the parallel thread. BSPT is the taper thread. Both use a 55 degree Whitworth form. For a phone or tablet in the shop, next to the NPT chart.

Quick rule: The nominal size is not the diameter across the threads. A 1/2 BSP male thread measures about 0.82 in and has 14 threads per inch. Eaton's chart calls that dash -8. The thread angle is 55 degrees, not the 60 degrees of NPT. A 1/4 BSP thread is 19 threads per inch. A 1/4 NPT thread is 18.

Parallel (BSPP, G) and taper (BSPT, R, Rp, Rc)

BSPP means British Standard Pipe Parallel. BSPT means British Standard Pipe Taper. They share the 55 degree Whitworth form and, at the same nominal size, the same basic major diameter and the same threads per inch. The difference is whether the thread is straight and where the joint seals.

ISO 228-1 is the parallel thread. The ISO page for that standard says both the internal and the external threads are parallel, and that they are not suitable where a pressure-tight joint is made on the thread. If the joint has to hold pressure, the seal is made by compressing two surfaces outside the threads and putting a seal between them. The designation letter is G. Eaton says the letter G means a parallel thread. Parker lists ISO 228/1, DIN 3852 Part 2, JIS B0202, and BS 2779 as the standards equivalent to BSPP. Swagelok calls ISO 228/1 BSPP, and also JIS B0202.

Parker describes two BSPP port faces. Form A uses a self-centering taper at the hex to locate a bonded washer, usually metal and elastomer, against the flat surface around the female thread. That bonded washer is the part sold as a Dowty seal. Form B uses a metal gasket, usually copper, between the two faces. The same Parker section says a parallel thread is for holding power and can also seal with an O-ring and a retainer ring. Swagelok describes the same family of seals on ISO 228/1 ends: a 30 degree cone on a swivel, a gasket compressed on a counterbore (DIN EN 837), a bonded elastomer gasket or a metal gasket on a DIN 3852 shoulder, and an O-ring compressed into a taper (BS 5380). Eaton says the female BSPP swivel has a tapered nose that seals on the cone seat of the male. Swagelok says a sealant is not required on straight threads, because the flanks do not wedge together. Thread lubricant is a separate question from the seal.

ISO 7-1 is the thread that is meant to seal on the flanks. Parker lists ISO 7/1, DIN 2999, JIS B0203, and BS 21 as equivalent to BSPT, and lists the taper angle as 1 degree 47 minutes, the thread angle as 55 degrees, and the crest and root as round. Swagelok says ISO 7/1 is also known as EN 10226-1 and JIS B0203, with the same 1 degree 47 minute taper, rounded crests and roots, a 55 degree thread angle, and pitch counted in threads per inch. A Power Transmission article on pipe-thread standards, citing ISO 7-1, BS EN 10226-1, and BS EN 10226-2, uses three letters for that series. R is the external taper. Rp is the internal parallel thread. Rc is the internal taper. BS EN 10226-1 is the taper-external and parallel-internal combination. BS EN 10226-2 is taper external and taper internal. Eaton says the letter R means a tapered thread, and gives the example R 3/8 for BSPT 3/8-19.

An R male is made to screw into Rp or into Rc. The seal is on the threads. Eaton says BSPT seals by distorting the threads and recommends a thread sealant. Swagelok marks the BSPT seal location as "on threads (sealant required)" and says tapered pipe threads need a sealant to hold the fluid and to limit galling. The Power Transmission article says the same for the R, Rp, and Rc series: a thread sealant is required for a pressure-tight joint. Do not leave the sealant off because the taper feels tight.

Swagelok's ISO 7/1 size list runs 1/16-28, 1/8-28, 1/4-19, 3/8-19, 1/2-14, 3/4-14, 1-11, 1-1/4-11, 1-1/2-11, and 2-11. It does not list 5/8, 7/8, 1-1/8, or 1-3/4. Those four are in Swagelok's ISO 228/1 tables. Eaton's combined BSP identification chart does include 5/8-14, so a fitting marked 5/8 BSP still has to be checked for a taper before you treat it as parallel only. G and Rp can both be parallel female threads of the same nominal size. The Power Transmission article says their pitch-diameter tolerances are not the same, so a G male is not a guaranteed substitute for a thread that was cut as Rp.

How to measure an unknown BSP fitting

  1. Look for a taper. On a male BSPT end the thread gets smaller toward the tip. A BSPP male thread stays the same diameter from the first full thread to the last. JIC, ORFS, and O-ring boss are straight threads as well, but they are 60 degree Unified threads, not 55 degree Whitworth.
  2. Look at the seal. A bonded washer or an O-ring at a shoulder, or a 30 degree cone on a swivel, is a parallel joint. A taper with nothing but thread sealant is BSPT. The thread still has to match the chart. The seal tells you which family you are in.
  3. Measure the male outside diameter across the crests. On a taper, use the large end, next to the hex. A reading at the tip is smaller than the basic major diameter. For a female thread, measure the inside diameter.
  4. Count threads per inch with a 55 degree pitch gauge. The pitches on this chart are 28, 19, 14, and 11. A 60 degree gauge can sit wrong on a Whitworth thread and call the pitch off by one.
  5. Match both numbers to the chart. A male thread near 0.82 in with 14 threads per inch is 1/2 BSP. A male thread near 1.04 in is 3/4 BSP, also 14 threads per inch. Then decide parallel or taper from the first two steps.

BSP is not interchangeable with NPT

NPT is American National Standard Taper Pipe Thread, a 60 degree form with flat crests and roots. BSP is a 55 degree form with round crests and roots. Parker says BSPT will not substitute for NPT. Both tapers are described as 1 degree 47 minutes: Parker lists that angle for NPT and for BSPT, and Swagelok lists it for ASME B1.20.1 and for ISO 7/1. The same taper angle does not make the threads the same.

The pitches differ on most sizes. Swagelok prints the pairs side by side: 1/16 is 27 for NPT and 28 for ISO 7/1, 1/8 is 27 and 28, 1/4 is 18 and 19, 3/8 is 18 and 19, 1 inch is 11-1/2 and 11, and 1-1/4, 1-1/2, and 2 follow that same 11-1/2 versus 11 split. The 1/2 and 3/4 sizes are 14 threads per inch in both families. Swagelok says those two sizes are hard to separate from NPT without an optical comparator, because the diameters are close. The table uses the basic major diameter from this chart and the basic outside diameter from the NPT chart.

FAQ

What is the difference between BSPP and BSPT?

BSPP is the parallel British pipe thread. The diameter stays the same along the thread, and the thread does not make the pressure seal. The Parker instrumentation manual says a parallel thread is for holding the joint, and the seal is a bonded washer, usually metal and elastomer, or an O-ring and retainer ring. ISO 228-1 names this thread G. BSPT is the taper British pipe thread. The Parker manual lists a 55 degree thread angle, round crests and roots, and a taper of 1 degree 47 minutes, and it lists ISO 7/1 among the equivalent standards. Eaton says the seal is made by distorting the threads and that a thread sealant is recommended. The external taper is named R.

What do G, R, Rp, and Rc mean?

G is the parallel thread in ISO 228-1, used on the male and on the female. Eaton says the letter G means parallel and the letter R means tapered. A published comparison of the pipe-thread standards says ISO 7-1, BS EN 10226-1, and BS EN 10226-2 use R for the external taper, Rp for the internal parallel thread, and Rc for the internal taper. An R male is the thread made to mate with Rp or Rc. A G female is parallel as well, but it is not the same specification as Rp.

Are BSP and NPT threads interchangeable?

No. The Parker instrumentation manual says BSPT threads will not substitute for NPT threads. NPT has a 60 degree thread angle and flat crests and roots. BSP has a 55 degree thread angle and round crests and roots. The pitches also differ on most sizes. A 1/4 BSP thread is 19 threads per inch and a 1/4 NPT thread is 18. A 1 inch BSP thread is 11 threads per inch and a 1 inch NPT thread is 11-1/2. The 1/2 and 3/4 sizes are both 14 threads per inch, so a pitch gauge will not split them. The major diameters are close, and the thread form is the difference.

Why does a 1/2 BSP fitting not measure 1/2 inch?

The 1/2 is the nominal pipe size, not the thread diameter. The basic major diameter for 1/2 BSP is 20.955 mm, which is 0.825 in, with 14 threads per inch. Eaton's identification chart lists the male outside diameter as 0.82 in. Eaton prints the dash for that row as 08. This chart writes it as dash -8.

How do I identify a BSP fitting size?

Measure the male thread outside diameter with calipers and count the threads per inch with a 55 degree pitch gauge. Then match both numbers to the chart. On a taper, measure near the large end. Pitch alone is not enough, because 1/16 and 1/8 are both 28 threads per inch, 1/4 and 3/8 are both 19, several sizes are 14, and 1 inch through 2 inch are 11. A straight thread with a bonded washer or an O-ring at the shoulder is BSPP. A thread that gets smaller toward the tip is BSPT. Check it is not NPT before you order a fitting. NPT uses a 60 degree form, and several sizes use a different pitch.
