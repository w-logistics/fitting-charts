NPT Pipe Thread Size Chart (ASME B1.20.1)

Nominal pipe sizes from 1/16″ through 2″, with threads per inch and the outside diameter people measure to name the size. NPT is American National Standard Taper Pipe Thread. For a phone or tablet in the shop, next to the JIC 37° chart and the ORFS chart.

Quick rule: The nominal size is not the diameter across the threads. A 1/2 NPT male thread measures about 0.84 in and has 14 threads per inch. Catalogs call that dash -8. A -8 JIC fitting is a different thread, 3/4-16, for 1/2″ tube.

Basic outside diameter is column D of the ASME B1.20.1 basic-dimension table, the outside diameter of the pipe the thread is based on. The millimeter column is that inch figure times 25.4, rounded to two decimal places (a remainder of 5 in the next digit is rounded up). It is not a separate metric standard.

The approximate male OD and female ID are the decimals in Eaton’s NPTF identification chart (How to Identify Fluid Ports and Connectors, E-SROV-TS009-E1, March 2013, page 8). Eaton also prints a fraction on the same row. Use the decimal. The fraction is not always the same number: the 1/4 row prints 17/32 and .54, and 17/32 in is 0.531 in. The decimal is the one that sits next to the basic outside diameter. Measure near the large end of the male thread. A reading further down the taper runs smaller, because the diameter changes along the thread.

Dash numbers are the nominal pipe size in sixteenths of an inch. Half-inch pipe is 8/16, so it is dash -8. Eaton prints 02, 04, 06, 08, 12, 16, 20, 24, and 32, without a leading dash, and does not list 1/16. Parker Catalog 4900 polyflex uses the same series as an NPT connection-size code, and it includes 1 for 1/16-27. This chart writes those codes as -1, -2, -4, and so on, the same way the JIC chart writes a dash size.

Eaton’s chart says to subtract 1/4 in from the measured thread diameter to estimate the nominal pipe size. That gets you close (0.84 minus 0.25 is 0.59, which points at 1/2 rather than 3/8 or 3/4). It is not exact. Confirm with the pitch and the row above.

Thread form: 60 degrees and a 1:16 taper

Parker’s instrumentation installation manual lists four traits of NPT: pitch measured in inches, flat truncation at the root and crest, a 60° thread angle, and a taper angle of 1° 47'. The same manual says Parker instrumentation NPT threads meet ANSI B1.20.1.

The taper those sources describe is 1 in 16 on the diameter. A published summary of ASME B1.20.1 states the rate as 1/16 (3/4 in per foot), measured as the change in diameter along the thread, and gives the angle between the taper and the centerline of the pipe as 1° 47' 24". Over one inch of thread length, the diameter changes by 1/16 in.

How to measure an unknown NPT fitting

  1. Look for a taper. On a male NPT end the thread gets smaller toward the tip. JIC, ORFS, and O-ring boss are straight threads. A straight thread with an O-ring or a flare is not NPT.
  2. Measure the male outside diameter near the large end, next to the hex or the shoulder. Do not use the first thread at the tip. For a female port, measure the inside diameter of the thread.
  3. Count threads per inch with a pitch gauge. The pitches on this chart are 27, 18, 14, and 11-1/2. Several sizes share a pitch, so the diameter still has to match.
  4. Match both numbers to the chart. A male thread near 0.84 in with 14 threads per inch is 1/2 NPT. A male thread near 1.05 in is 3/4 NPT, also 14 threads per inch.
  5. Rule out BSPT before you order a fitting. The pitches and the thread angle are different. The comparison is in the next sections.

NPT vs NPTF (Dryseal)

NPT is ASME B1.20.1, general purpose taper pipe thread. NPTF is Dryseal American National Standard Taper Pipe Thread, ASME B1.20.3. Parker’s port assembly guide and Catalog 4300 Table E1 also cite SAE J476 for NPTF.

The nominal sizes, the threads per inch, and the taper are the same family of sizes. The crest and root are not cut the same way. Parker Catalog 4300, in the pipe fittings introduction, says an NPT joint assembled without sealant leaves a spiral leak path where the crest and root do not touch, so NPT needs a sealant. NPTF is truncated so the crest of one thread meets the root of the mating thread at hand tight (Parker’s Fig. E2), and further tightening brings the flanks into contact as well (Fig. E3). That is the dryseal idea: a metal-to-metal seal without a gap along the spiral.

Parker says that idea does not survive real parts. Variation in the mating threads, materials, and how the joint is assembled makes a true dry seal unlikely, so Parker still calls for a thread sealant on NPTF, and sometimes a lubricant to limit galling. Catalog 4300 also says most steel and brass pipe fittings in that section are cut with NPTF, while stainless parts may be NPT, or a slightly modified NPT, to reduce galling.

Eaton’s identification page describes NPTF the same way: the thread is tapered and the seal is made by deformation of the threads. Eaton also says the National Fluid Power Association does not recommend NPTF for hydraulic applications. Parker’s pipe section makes a related point: tapered pipe threads are a poor choice where the joint is opened often, or where pressure pulses and vibration are part of the job. For those systems Parker points to a port with an elastomeric seal, such as SAE straight thread or a four-bolt flange. This chart identifies the thread. It is not a pressure rating.

Parker’s port guide also says not to loosen a tapered pipe fitting to aim an elbow. Backing it off to line up the tube or hose opens the seal. Use the fitting maker’s turns-from-finger-tight for the size you have. Parker prints those turns in Table R5 of the Port End Assembly Guide.

NPT is not interchangeable with BSPT

BSPT is British Standard Pipe Taper. Parker’s instrumentation manual says BSPT will not substitute for NPT. NPT has a 60° thread angle and flat crests and roots. BSPT has a 55° thread angle and round truncation. Both have a taper angle of 1° 47'. Parker lists ISO 7-1, BS 21, DIN 2999, and JIS B0203 as the standards that match BSPT. Catalog 4300 says NPT/NPTF, BSPT, and metric taper differ in dimensions, pitch, and flank angle, and that those differences do not allow interchange.

Eaton’s guide says the BSPT connection is similar to NPT, except the pitches differ in most sizes, and the thread form and outside diameters are close but not the same. The table below uses Eaton’s identification decimals for both. Eaton’s NPT chart starts at 1/8, so 1/16 is not in this comparison. Eaton also lists a 5/8-14 BSP size (male OD 0.88 in). There is no 5/8 NPT row on the chart above.

A pitch gauge separates most of these pairs. 1/8 NPT is 27 threads per inch and 1/8 BSPT is 28. 1/4 NPT is 18 and 1/4 BSPT is 19. 1 in NPT is 11-1/2 and 1 in BSPT is 11. The 1/2 and 3/4 sizes are both 14 threads per inch, so the pitch gauge will not split them. The outside diameter is close, and the 60° versus 55° form is the difference a pitch gauge does not show. If the fitting came off imported equipment, do not assume the port is NPT because the size stamp says 1/2.

Parallel BSPP uses the Whitworth form and, on the sizes both series list, the same pitch as BSPT. It seals on a washer or an O-ring instead of on the threads. Sizes, major diameters, and the G, R, Rp, and Rc names are on the BSPP and BSPT thread size chart.

FAQ

How do I identify an NPT fitting size?

Measure the male thread near its large end, toward the wrench flats, with calipers. Count the threads per inch with a pitch gauge. Then match both numbers to the chart. Pitch alone is not enough, because 1/8 and 1/16 are both 27 threads per inch, 1/4 and 3/8 are both 18, 1/2 and 3/4 are both 14, and 1 in through 2 in are all 11-1/2. The thread is tapered, so a reading at the small end is smaller than the basic outside diameter.

What is the difference between NPT and NPTF?

NPT is the general purpose American National Standard Taper Pipe Thread, ASME B1.20.1. NPTF is Dryseal taper pipe thread, ASME B1.20.3, and Parker also cites SAE J476 for it. The nominal sizes, threads per inch, and taper match. The crest and root do not. Parker’s pipe fitting section says an NPT joint assembled without sealant leaves a spiral leak path at the crest and root, so NPT needs a sealant. NPTF is shaped so the crest of one thread can meet the root of the other. Parker says that in real fittings a sealant is still used on NPTF, because threads and assembly conditions are not perfect. Eaton notes that the National Fluid Power Association does not recommend NPTF for hydraulic applications.

Are NPT and BSPT interchangeable?

No. Parker’s instrumentation manual says BSPT threads will not substitute for NPT threads. NPT has a 60 degree thread angle and flat crests and roots. BSPT has a 55 degree thread angle and round crests and roots. Both have a taper angle of 1 degree 47 minutes. Eaton’s identification guide says the pitches differ in most sizes, and the outside diameters are close but not the same. A 1/4 NPT is 18 threads per inch. A 1/4 BSPT is 19. Sizes that share a pitch, such as 1/2-14 and 3/4-14, still differ in diameter and thread form.

Why does a 1/2 NPT fitting not measure 1/2 inch?

The name is the nominal pipe size, not the thread diameter. The basic outside diameter for 1/2 NPT is 0.840 in, and Eaton’s identification chart lists the male outside diameter as 0.84 in, with 14 threads per inch. Hydraulic catalogs call that size dash -8.

Is dash -8 NPT the same thread as dash -8 JIC?

No. The dash number is a size in sixteenths of an inch on both charts, but the thread is not the same standard. Dash -8 JIC is a straight 3/4-16 thread for 1/2 in tube. Dash -8 NPT is a tapered 1/2-14 pipe thread. Match the fitting family before you match the dash.
