DIN 2353 / ISO 8434-1 24° Cone Fitting Size Chart

Metric tube fittings with a 24° cone: extra light (LL), light (L), and heavy (S). Each row is the tube outside diameter, the metric nut thread, and the hex Parker prints for that size. For a phone or tablet in the shop, next to the JIC 37° chart and the ORFS chart.

Quick rule: The size is the tube outside diameter in millimeters plus the series letter. 12L is light series for 12 mm tube, thread M18 x 1.5. 12S is heavy series for the same tube, thread M20 x 1.5. The thread is straight and metric. It is not a JIC inch thread, and the seat is 24°, not 37°.

The nut thread and the nut hex are the tube-end thread and the across-flats size Parker prints for the M tube nut. Catalog 4300 (PDF version 12, November 2025) calls that column S1 on the M nut page and S2, tube/swivel nut hex, on the tube-end reference chart. Catalogue 4100-8/UK prints the same S1 on its M nut page. Where both catalogs print a nut hex, the numbers match. 5LL is not on either nut page.

The union body hex is S1 on the Parker G straight union, the body across flats. Catalog 4300 and Catalogue 4100-8/UK print the same body hex on every shared row. 14S is only on the Catalogue 4100 union page, at 24 mm. The column is the straight union only. An elbow or a tee in the same catalog uses a different body hex for the same tube size. Use the nut when you are naming the size. Use the body hex only when the part in your hand is a straight union.

5LL is in ISO 8434-1:2018 Table 1, thread M10 x 1, 100 bar. Parker Catalogue 4100 lists LL tube sizes as 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12 mm, and Catalog 4300 has no 5 mm nut, so both hex cells stay blank. 10LL and 12LL are on the Parker nut and union pages. They are not rows in the Table 1 preview used here, so the pressure cell says not listed. 14S is on the Catalogue 4100 M nut page (27 mm) and G union page (body 24 mm, nut 27 mm), and on the Catalog 4300 tube-end reference chart (M22 x 1.5, nut hex 27 mm). The Catalog 4300 M nut order table and G union order table skip 14S, and Table 1 does not list it. The thread and the 27 mm nut are the figures both Parker sources print. The 24 mm body is the Catalogue 4100 straight union.

The bar column is Table 1 of ISO 8434-1:2018 for the cone and cutting-ring connection. The standard states a design factor of 4 to 1, carbon steel, petroleum-based fluid, from -40 to +120 degrees C. It also says the figures are not guaranteed minimums, and that the manufacturer sets the rating for stainless steel and for pressures above the table. Parker prints a separate deliverable pressure for each material on the nut page. Those Parker numbers are not this column. This page identifies the fitting. It is not a pressure rating for a brand, a material, or a hose.

Same thread, two tube sizes

M14 x 1.5 is the one that gets ordered wrong most often: 8L and 6S, both with a 17 mm nut and, on the Parker straight union, a 14 mm body. A 6 mm tube on that thread is 6S. An 8 mm tube is 8L. M36 x 2 is the overlap where the nut hex does change: 41 mm on 28L and 46 mm on 25S. The straight-union body on that pair is 41 mm for both, so the body hex still does not split them. What you are looking at

ISO 8434-1 is the 24° cone connector. The scope covers a cutting ring and an O-ring seal cone, which the standard calls DKO, for tube from 4 mm to 42 mm. The standard names three series: LL extra light-duty, L light-duty, and S heavy-duty. DIN 2353 is the older national standard Parker still names. Catalogue 4100 says EO fittings follow DIN EN ISO 8434-1, formerly DIN 2353 and DIN 3861.

A cutting-ring joint is three pieces: a body with a 24° cone, a ring that bites the outside of the tube, and a nut that drives the ring into the cone. Parker calls the current ring a progressive ring. The bite is the seal. EO-2 uses the same cone and the same series, and adds an elastomer in the joint. A DKO swivel is the other seal in the standard: a male 24° cone with an O-ring, held by a swivel nut. The size chart is the same for all three. The ring, the elastomer, or the O-ring on the cone tells you which version you have. It does not change the thread.

The thread in this chart is the nut and the cone end. A stud that screws into a port is often a different thread. ISO 8434-1 Table 1 prints both. On the 12L row the cone thread is M18 x 1.5 and the ISO 6149 stud on that same row is M16 x 1.5. BSPP studs show up on DIN fittings as well. Match the end you are replacing. Do not order the port thread as if it were the tube nut.

How to measure an unknown 24° fitting

  1. Look at the seat. The body has a 24° cone, and a cutting ring sits on the tube, or a male cone carries an O-ring (DKO). A 37° flare with an inch thread is JIC. A flat face with an O-ring groove and an inch thread is ORFS. A taper thread is NPT or BSPT, not this chart.
  2. Measure the tube outside diameter in millimeters, on a straight section, not on the bite. Common light-series tubes are 6, 8, 10, 12, 15, and 18. Heavy series adds 16, 20, 25, 30, and 38, and Parker also lists 14.
  3. Measure the male thread across the crests. Metric major diameters on this chart run from 8 mm to 52 mm. Then check the pitch: 1 mm, 1.5 mm, or 2 mm. A 60° metric pitch gauge is the right leaf. An inch gauge will not name these pitches.
  4. Match thread and tube together. M18 x 1.5 with 12 mm tube is 12L. The same thread with 10 mm tube is 10S. If the tube is missing, the nut hex narrows it, and on M36 x 2 the hex splits 28L from 25S. On the other shared threads the nut hex does not split them.
  5. Read the series off the part if it is stamped. Parker stamps the tube size and the letter, such as 12L or 12S. ISO examples write the letter first, such as S12. Either form is the same size.

24° cone, JIC 37°, and ORFS

Parker Catalogue 4100 puts the three fittings on one comparison page. EO cutting-ring and EO-2 ends are ISO 8434-1, the old DIN 2353 and DIN 3861, metric tube, 24° cone. Triple-Lok is the 37° flare, ISO 8434-2 and SAE J514, metal seal on the flare. O-Lok is the O-ring face seal, ISO 8434-3 and SAE J1453, elastomer on a flat face. The JIC chart is the SAE J514 inch series. The ORFS chart is the SAE J1453 face seal. The threads on those two charts are inch threads. Nothing on this chart is an inch thread.

Do not start a 24° nut onto a 37° flare because both ends look conical. The angles do not meet. Do not use an ORFS flat-face swivel on a 24° cone. The ORFS seal is an O-ring crushed between two flat faces, and the thread that pulls those faces together is not the metric thread in the table above. A DKO O-ring can look a bit like an ORFS O-ring if you only glance at it. DKO puts the O-ring on the cone. ORFS puts the O-ring in a groove on a flat nose, in an inch thread.

FAQ

How do I identify a 24° cone fitting size?

Measure the tube outside diameter with calipers. Measure the male thread across the crests, and match the pitch with a metric pitch gauge. The pitches on this chart are 1 mm, 1.5 mm, and 2 mm. Then match the thread and the tube diameter to one row. The thread is not enough on its own. M14 x 1.5 is both 8L and 6S, and those two rows also share the 17 mm nut. The tube diameter is what separates them. A 24° cone in the body, or a cutting ring on the tube, is this family. A 37° flare is JIC. A flat face with an O-ring groove is ORFS.

What do LL, L, and S mean?

ISO 8434-1 uses LL for extra light-duty, L for light-duty, and S for heavy-duty. Parker Catalogue 4100 calls the same three series very light, light, and heavy. The letter is part of the size. 12L is the light series for 12 mm tube, thread M18 x 1.5. 12S is the heavy series for the same tube, thread M20 x 1.5. Parker order codes put the letter after the tube size (M12L, M12S). An ISO 8434-1 ordering example puts the letter first (S12). Those are the same size, written two ways.

How does a 24° cone fitting differ from JIC and ORFS?

The seal and the thread are both different. A DIN 2353 / ISO 8434-1 joint uses a metric straight thread and a 24° cone. A cutting ring bites the tube and seats in that cone, or a DKO end puts an O-ring on a 24° male cone. Parker Catalogue 4100 lists its 37° flare as Triple-Lok, with ISO 8434-2 and SAE J514, and a metal-to-metal seal. That is the fitting on the JIC chart, and the thread is an inch thread, not a metric thread. The same catalogue lists O-Lok as an O-ring face seal, ISO 8434-3 and SAE J1453, with the seal on a flat face. That is the fitting on the ORFS chart. A 24° cone does not seat on a 37° flare, and it does not seal on an ORFS face.

Why do some threads appear twice on the chart?

The heavy series uses a larger thread for the same tube than the light series does, so a light size and a smaller heavy size can land on one thread. M18 x 1.5 is 12L and 10S. M30 x 2 is 22L and 20S. M52 x 2 is 42L and 38S. On those pairs the Parker nut hex matches too, except M36 x 2: the 28L nut is 41 mm and the 25S nut is 46 mm. M10 x 1 is 6LL, and ISO 8434-1 Table 1 also lists 5 mm extra light on that same thread. Read the tube diameter before you order the nut.

Is the pressure column a rating for the fitting in my hand?

No. The pressure column is the ISO 8434-1:2018 Table 1 working pressure for the cone and cutting-ring connection, for carbon steel, with a design factor of 4 to 1. The standard says those figures are not guaranteed minimums, and it says to ask the manufacturer for higher pressures and for dynamic conditions. Stainless steel ratings are set by the manufacturer. Parker prints a deliverable pressure for each material on the nut page, and those numbers are not this column. A cell that says not listed was not in the source for that column. 5LL has no Parker hex. 10LL, 12LL, and 14S are not rows in the Table 1 preview, so this chart gives them no ISO pressure.
