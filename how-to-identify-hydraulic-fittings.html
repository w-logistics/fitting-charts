How to Identify Hydraulic Fittings: JIC, ORFS, NPT, BSP, ORB, and Metric

A shop guide for naming the fitting in your hand. Look at the seal first, then measure the thread. The size charts linked below hold the full tables. This page is how to pick the right chart.

Quick rule: A 37° flare is JIC. An O-ring in a flat face is ORFS. An O-ring at the shoulder of a straight inch thread is ORB. A thread that gets smaller toward the tip is NPT or BSPT. A parallel pipe thread with a bonded washer or a 30° cone is BSPP. A 24° cone on a metric thread is the DIN fitting. A flat pad with four bolt holes and no thread is a split flange. Code 61 and Code 62 are on the flange chart.

How to tell the families apart

The thread holds many of these joints. It does not always make the seal. Two fittings can share a thread and still leak if you mate the wrong seats. Read the seal column before you match a thread callout.

What separates the common hydraulic ends. Pitch is the series used on the size charts, not one pitch for every size.

JIC (SAE J514) 37° flare Straight Unified inch thread. Parker shows that screw thread at 60°. Parallel 24, 20, 18, 16, 14, or 12 TPI Metal to metal on the flare. The threads hold the joint.
ORFS (SAE J1453) Flat face, no cone Straight Unified inch thread. Sizes differ from JIC at the same dash. Parallel 18, 16, 14, or 12 TPI O-ring compressed between two flat faces. The threads hold the joint.
ORB (SAE J1926) No cone on the male. O-ring at the shoulder. Same straight Unified thread as JIC at the same dash. Parallel Same TPI series as JIC O-ring compressed into the port chamfer. The threads hold the joint.
NPT and NPTF No seat angle 60° pipe thread, flat crest and root. NPT is ASME B1.20.1. NPTF is ASME B1.20.3. Taper, 1 degree 47 minutes 27, 18, 14, or 11-1/2 TPI On the threads. Eaton describes NPTF as sealing by deformation of the threads.
BSPP (ISO 228-1) 30° cone on a swivel, or a flat face for a washer or O-ring 55° Whitworth, round crest and root Parallel 28, 19, 14, or 11 TPI Outside the threads. Parker lists a bonded washer, a metal gasket, or an O-ring.

NPT and NPTF share the nominal sizes, the threads per inch, and the taper, so they share one column. The crest and root are not cut the same way. The NPT chart covers that difference, and Eaton notes that the National Fluid Power Association does not recommend NPTF for hydraulic applications. This page names the thread. It is not a pressure rating.

BSPP and BSPT share the 55° form, the threads per inch, and the basic major diameter at the same nominal size. G is the parallel thread. R is the external taper. A straight thread with a bonded washer, a copper gasket, or an O-ring at the shoulder is BSPP. Parker calls the bonded washer Form A and the metal gasket Form B. Swagelok describes the tube swivel as a 30° taper on the male against a 30° cone in the nut. A thread that shrinks toward the tip, with nothing but sealant, is BSPT.

JIC and ORB are the pair that share a thread on purpose. The ORB chart prints the port chamfer as 12° on dash -2 through -6 and 15° on dash -8 through -32, from Parker’s port table. That angle is the port, not a flare you measure on the male nose. The O-ring sits at the shoulder, under the hex or under a locknut.

Eaton also lists NPSM, a straight American pipe thread with an inverted 30° seat. The thread callouts and male diameters in that table match the NPT sizes. The thread is straight, not tapered. It is not BSPP. BSPP is the 55° Whitworth thread, and most sizes use a different pitch.

How to measure thread OD and pitch

Eaton’s identification guide uses three tools: a caliper, a pitch gauge, and a seat-angle gauge. Swagelok’s thread guide uses the same idea and writes the order below. A reading will not always land on the exact table number. Swagelok says manufacturing tolerance is the reason. Match the closest row, then check the seal.

  1. Straight or taper. With calipers, measure the crest-to-crest diameter on the first, fourth, and last full threads. On a male end, if the diameter grows toward the hex, the thread is tapered. If the three readings match, the thread is straight. JIC, ORFS, ORB, BSPP, and the DIN cone nut are straight. NPT, NPTF, and BSPT are tapered.
  2. Outside diameter. Measure crest to crest. On a straight male thread, any full thread is fine. On a taper, do not use the tip. Swagelok says to measure the fourth or fifth full thread. The NPT and BSP charts say to measure near the large end, next to the hex, and to compare that reading with Eaton’s male outside diameter. A reading at the small end runs under the chart.
  3. Female threads. Measure the inside diameter across the crests. Eaton’s charts print a female ID next to the male OD. Use the column on the chart for that family. A female NPT or BSPT port is larger at the face and smaller deeper in.
  4. Pitch. Set a pitch-gauge leaf on the threads until it sits snug, with no light under the leaf. Count threads per inch on Unified and pipe threads. On a metric thread, the pitch is the distance in millimeters from crest to crest. Use a 60° leaf for JIC, ORB, ORFS, and NPT. Use a 55° Whitworth leaf for BSP. A 60° leaf is the wrong form for a Whitworth thread. Use a metric leaf for the DIN cone. The pitches on that chart are 1 mm, 1.5 mm, and 2 mm.
  5. Seat angle. On a male flare, lay the angle gauge on the sealing surface. Eaton says the angle matches when the centerline of the fitting and the centerline of the gauge are parallel. Swagelok’s set includes 37°, 45°, and 30° gauges, and it notes that other seat angles exist. A 24° cone will not sit parallel on a 37° or 30° gauge. An O-ring in the face means you are not measuring a flare.

Identification flow

  1. Look at the seal before you measure. 37° cone and no O-ring: JIC. O-ring in a groove on a flat nose: ORFS. O-ring at the shoulder of a straight inch thread: ORB. Thread smaller at the tip, no separate seat: NPT or BSPT. Parallel pipe thread with a bonded washer, or a 30° cone on a swivel: BSPP. 24° cone, metric thread, cutting ring or an O-ring on the cone: DIN 2353. O-ring at the shoulder of a metric thread, sealing in a port: ISO 6149, not the 24° nut.
  2. Confirm straight or taper with the three crest readings above. A taper rules out JIC, ORFS, ORB, BSPP, and the DIN cone nut.
  3. Measure the diameter and the pitch and take both numbers to the table below. Pitch alone is not enough. Several sizes in one family share a pitch, and a few threads are shared by two families.
  4. If two families can claim the thread, trust the seal. JIC and ORB use the same thread at the same dash. 9/16-18 is -6 JIC and -4 ORFS. 1/2-14 is both 1/2 NPT and 1/2 BSP. The seat, the O-ring, or the 60° versus 55° form is what splits them.
  5. Open the chart for that family and match the full row before you order. The charts on this site are the size tables. This page is the path into them.

Threads that sit close together

  • 9/16-18, nominal major 0.5625 in. That is JIC and ORB dash -6 (3/8 in tube) and also ORFS dash -4 (1/4 in tube). A 37° cone is JIC. An O-ring at the shoulder is ORB. An O-ring in the flat face is ORFS.
  • 3/4-16, nominal major 0.7500 in. That is JIC and ORB dash -8. ORFS dash -8 is 13/16-16, nominal major 0.8125 in. Eaton also lists 3/4-16 for SAE 45° dash -8. The 45° seat is not a 37° seat. Eaton says the 37° and 45° threads match on dash -2, -3, -4, -5, -8, and -10, and the seat angles do not.
  • 1/2-14. NPT male OD is 0.84 in. BSP male OD is 0.82 in. Both are 14 threads per inch, so a pitch gauge will not split them. The 60° form against the 55° form is the check. Swagelok says 1/2 and 3/4 are hard to separate from NPT without an optical comparator, because the diameters are close. The same is true of 3/4-14: NPT male OD 1.05 in, BSP male OD 1.04 in.
  • About 1.31 in at 1 inch nominal. JIC and ORB dash -16 are 1-5/16-12, nominal major 1.3125 in, 12 TPI. NPT is 1-11 1/2, male OD 1.32 in. BSP is 1-11, male OD 1.30 in. The pitch is what separates them: 12, 11-1/2, or 11.
  • About 1.05 in at 3/4 inch nominal. JIC and ORB dash -12 are 1-1/16-12, nominal major 1.0625 in, 12 TPI. NPT 3/4 is 14 TPI and male OD 1.05 in. BSP 3/4 is 14 TPI and male OD 1.04 in. Count the pitch before you call a 1.05 in thread a pipe thread.

Metric: 24° cone and O-ring port

A metric thread is named by the major diameter and the pitch in millimeters. M18 x 1.5 is an 18 mm major diameter and a 1.5 mm pitch. An inch pitch gauge will not name it. Parker’s instrumentation manual says the metric pitch is the crest-to-crest distance in millimeters.

The hydraulic tube fitting in this family is the 24° cone, DIN 2353 and ISO 8434-1. Eaton’s DIN 3902 section describes the male as a straight metric thread with a 24° included angle. The female may be a nut and cutting ring, a swivel with a tapered nose, or that swivel with an O-ring in the nose (DKO). The full size table, including the Parker nut hex, is on the DIN chart. Three rows that match both that chart and Eaton:

  • 12L: 12 mm tube, light series, M18 x 1.5
  • 12S: 12 mm tube, heavy series, M20 x 1.5
  • 8L and 6S: both M14 x 1.5. The tube diameter is what splits them. 8 mm is 8L. 6 mm is 6S.

ISO 6149 is a different metric end. Eaton describes it the way it describes ORB: a straight thread, an O-ring, and a port chamfer. The threads hold the joint. The O-ring seals. M18 x 1.5 is both the 12L cone thread and an ISO 6149 stud size in Eaton’s port table, with a male outside diameter of 18 mm. Look at the end. A 24° cone is the tube nut. An O-ring at the shoulder is the port stud. Do not order one as the other.

Do not start a 24° nut onto a 37° flare. The angles do not meet. Do not use an ORFS flat-face swivel on a 24° cone. ORFS is an inch thread and a flat face. DKO puts the O-ring on the 24° cone.

FAQ

How do I tell JIC from ORFS?

JIC seals metal to metal on a 37° flare. There is no O-ring. ORFS seals with an O-ring in a groove on a flat face, pulled against a flat swivel. The thread at the same dash is not the same. A -8 JIC is 3/4-16. A -8 ORFS is 13/16-16. Some callouts show up in both families at different dashes. 9/16-18 is -6 JIC and -4 ORFS. Do not seat a JIC cone on an ORFS face.

How do I tell NPT from BSP?

NPT has a 60° thread angle and flat crests and roots. BSP has a 55° thread angle and round crests and roots. Parker Bulletin 4200-B4 says BSPT will not substitute for NPT. The pitch differs on most sizes. 1/8 NPT is 27 threads per inch and 1/8 BSP is 28. 1/4 NPT is 18 and 1/4 BSP is 19. 1 inch NPT is 11-1/2 and 1 inch BSP is 11. The 1/2 and 3/4 sizes are 14 threads per inch in both families. Eaton's male outside diameter is 0.84 in for 1/2 NPT and 0.82 in for 1/2 BSP. Swagelok says those two sizes are hard to separate without an optical comparator. A parallel thread with a bonded washer or a 30° cone is BSPP, not NPT.

JIC and ORB use the same thread. How do I tell them apart?

The thread matches and the seal does not. A -8 JIC and a -8 ORB are both 3/4-16 for 1/2 in tube. JIC has a 37° cone and seals metal to metal. ORB has a straight thread with an O-ring at the shoulder, and that O-ring seals in the chamfer of a SAE J1926 port. A JIC male can start into an ORB port of the same dash and still leak, because the flare is not the seal for that port.

Why does the measured size not match the name on the fitting?

On JIC, ORB, and ORFS the dash is the tube outside diameter in sixteenths of an inch, not the thread diameter. Dash -8 is 1/2 in tube. The -8 JIC thread is 3/4-16, and the nominal major diameter is 0.7500 in. On NPT and BSP the name is the nominal pipe size. A 1/2 NPT male thread measures about 0.84 in, with 14 threads per inch. A male thread also measures a little under the nominal major, and a taper reads smaller toward the tip.

How do I tell a metric 24° fitting from JIC?

JIC is a 37° flare on a straight inch thread. A DIN 2353 fitting, the ISO 8434-1 24° cone, uses a metric straight thread. Eaton describes the male as a 24° included angle. A cutting ring bites the tube and seats in that cone, or a DKO end puts an O-ring on the cone. 12L is 12 mm tube and M18 x 1.5. 12S is the same tube and M20 x 1.5. The 24° cone does not seat on a 37° flare. An O-ring on the cone is not an ORFS face seal. ORFS puts the O-ring in a flat face on an inch thread.
